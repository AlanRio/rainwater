Will the rainwater last the summer?

An off-grid house near Prayssac (Lot, France) runs entirely on rain collected from its roofs. This page checks, day by day, whether its tanks can supply guests through the summer, using the rain that actually fell. The recommended fix is a rain sheet on the slope next to the house.

The recommended fix

A rain sheet on the slope next to the house

The house runs short because its roofs catch too little rain. With the house full all summer they can't even refill the tanks over winter, so each summer starts with the tanks part-empty, and summer showers add little. So the answer is more collecting surface. The simplest, cheapest and lowest-risk way to get it is one rubber (EPDM) sheet, about 20 × 8.75 m (175 m²), laid on the open slope of grass and low scrub just south of the buildings, draining into a 10 m³ buried tank at the end of its collection trench. The tank is what keeps the sheet this small: it holds the sheet's water from storms that come when the main tanks are already full, and without it the sheet would need to be about 230 m² for the same result. A standard precast concrete rainwater tank does the job; concrete releases nothing harmful into the water. A small solar pump moves its water into the existing underground tanks whenever they have room. If the main tanks turn out to hold about 10 m³ more than estimated, they do the same job and the extra tank isn't needed, so measure them first.

It's sized so that no tanker would have been needed in any summer since 2010. A summer as dry as 2009, the driest since 1994, would need one or two tankers; it's not worth a bigger sheet for that, and the sheet can be extended later if very dry summers become more common. A bigger buried tank lets the sheet shrink a little further.

Where it goes, from the LiDAR survey
  • The house sits on a saddle at about 229.5 m, between two hollows. Bowl 1, to the south-west, is a closed limestone hollow (a doline) with its floor at about 224.5 m. Bowl 2, to the north-east, is the head of a dry valley that falls away north.
  • The sheet goes on the slope just south of the buildings, at the south-east end of bowl 1. The ground there rises from about 231 to 233 m at a steady 10°, facing west-north-west. It's open ground of grass and low scrub; the trees start at the top of the slope, and a few bushes and small trees at the corners may need cutting back.
  • It's square on the slope. The 20 m edges run along the contour lines and the 8.75 m sides run straight down the slope, so every part of the sheet drains by the shortest route into one trench along the bottom edge.
  • Gravity does the work. The bottom edge, at about 231.2–231.6 m, is above the ground at the tanks (about 229.5 m, at the southern corner of the north-east building). About 25 m of buried pipe runs north from the outlet to the underground tanks and falls about 2 m. The ground rises about half a metre beside the south-east building, so that stretch of pipe trench is up to about 2 m deep, close to the building and past tree roots.
  • Hard to see. The sheet lies flat on the ground below the trees, so it shows little from a distance. A dark colour helps.

Heights are from the IGN LiDAR HD survey (geoservices.ign.fr/lidarhd), tiles 0557_6382 and 0558_6382, flown 11 April 2022.

How it's built
  1. Clear the ground between September and February, outside the bird nesting season and the spring flowering: cut the grass and bushes short and take out any woody roots that could push up under the sheet. No weedkiller.
  2. Smooth the ground. Rake off stones bigger than about 2 cm and fill hollows so no water can pond. Shape the surface as a very shallow trough, with the middle 10–20 cm lower than the ends, so water runs down the middle.
  3. Dig the collection trench along the bottom edge, following the contour, falling a steady 1% (20 cm over 20 m) to the outlet at the north-east end, nearest the tanks. The ground rises slightly towards that end, so the trench is about 30 cm deep at the south-west end and about 90 cm at the outlet. Line it with the bottom edge of the sheet.
  4. Put in the extra tank (see The 10 m³ extra tank, and checking the main tanks) next to the north-east end of the collection trench, with its inlet below the trench outlet so the sheet's water runs into it through a leaf screen.
  5. Dig anchor trenches about 30 × 30 cm along the top edge and both sides, and a shallow diversion ditch above the top edge that sends water and leaves from the wood round the sheet. Its ends run into the edge of the trees, so keep them shallow there and work round the roots.
  6. Lay a protective underlay of non-woven geotextile (300 g/m², or 500 g/m² over rock or a lot of stone).
  7. Lay the sheet from the top on a calm, warm day, when the rubber is supple. Pull it downhill so it lies flat without ripples. Bury its top and side edges in the anchor trenches and lap the bottom edge into the collection trench.
  8. Dig the pipe trench to the tanks, about 25 m, with a steady fall all the way. It's up to about 2 m deep where the ground rises beside the south-east building, so use the digger and keep clear of the foundations.
  9. Connect the extra tank to the main tanks: a small solar pump in the extra tank, switched by a float switch in the main tanks so it only pumps when they have room, and about 25 m of pipe in the pipe trench, with a self-cleaning leaf filter where it enters them.
  10. Fence it against wild boar and deer, and keep guests off: wet rubber on a slope is very slippery. Cut back any branches that overhang it.

Upkeep: sweep or blow off leaves before May and after summer storms, clean the leaf filter, and check the sheet once a year. Small tears are patched like a bicycle tyre.

Why EPDM. It's heavy enough that the wind doesn't lift it, lies flat on uneven ground, comes in one piece at this size so there's nothing to weld, and lasts 20–40 years in the sun. HDPE plastic is cheaper but stiff and wrinkles in the heat; PVC liners go brittle in sunlight within about 10 years. Rigid roofing sheets on a frame would last as long but cost about €6,000–10,000, catch the wind, show from further away, and count as a building, which this zone of the local plan doesn't allow at that size.

The 10 m³ extra tank, and checking the main tanks

Why a tank. A storm that arrives when the main tanks are already full would otherwise run off the sheet and be lost. The extra tank holds that water and passes it to the main tanks as they empty. That's what lets the sheet be 175 m² instead of about 230 m² for the same result. A bigger tank lets the sheet shrink a little more, but the gain tails off quickly:

Same result in each row: no tanker in any summer since 2010, and one or two in a summer as dry as 2009. The simulator models the tank as filled only by the sheet and emptying into the main tanks whenever they have room.

The trade-off between tank and sheet. More storage lets the sheet shrink a little, but only a little. Below about 155–175 m², storage can't make up for it: the sheet's area is what brings water in during the summer, and with too little of it the longer record shows tankers in several dry summers however big the tanks are. So plan on about 175 m² of sheet. The tank question is only how to provide enough storage in total.

Check the main tanks first

What matters is the total storage, not where it sits. A cubic metre in the main tanks is worth at least as much as one in the extra tank, because the main tanks also take the roof water. The page assumes the two main tanks, the house tank and the solar building tank, are 4 × 4 × 4 m each with 90% usable, which is 115 m³. That's an estimate: they haven't been measured, and the answer depends on it. With the 175 m² sheet feeding the main tanks directly, about 120–124 m³ of usable storage does the job of the extra tank:

So measure them before building anything. In each tank: the inside length and width, the depth from the floor to the overflow pipe, and the height of the pump intake above the floor (water below it can't be used). Usable volume = length × width × (overflow height − intake height). If the two together come to about 124 m³ or more, skip the extra tank. If they come up short, the extra tank only has to make up the difference. Small differences add up: an extra 25 cm of depth across both tanks is about 8 m³.

To check it in the simulator: enter the measured sizes under Tanks, set Usable share of the tank volume under Tanks in detail, tick the ground sheet, untick "Extra tank (sheet only)", and set Start from the year to 1994.

Size of the extra tank. It needs to hold x = 124 m³ minus the usable storage in the main tanks: 10 m³ with the current estimate of 115 m³. Buy one a little bigger than x, as the bottom and top of any tank can't be used, and round up to the next standard size.

It only empties when the main tanks have room. A float switch in the main tanks runs the pump only while there's room in them; otherwise the extra tank keeps its water. When both are full, the extra tank overflows onto the slope below. If a low, wide tank fits between the trench outlet (about 231 m) and the main tanks' inlet (about 229.3 m), gravity can replace the pump, with a float valve at the main tanks doing the same job as the float switch.

What to build it from:

  • Recommended: a precast concrete rainwater tank, buried, from a local concrete works; a 10 m³ tank is roughly 3.5 × 2.2 × 2 m. Concrete has been used to store drinking water for over a century and releases nothing harmful; it even nudges acidic rainwater's pH up a little. A new tank releases lime at first, which pushes the pH right up, so fill and empty it once or twice before use. Ask for one certified for drinking water (ACS) with no coating inside that isn't approved. It needs a crane or a large digger to lift it in.
  • Standard buried plastic rainwater tanks, two or three linked, if a crane can't reach the site. Choose food-grade or drinking-water-grade polyethylene.
  • Polyethylene drinking-water main pipe, 20 m of 900 mm pipe with welded end caps (about 10.8 m³), laid under the trench. It drains by gravity and needs no pump, but costs about €5,000–9,000. A concrete-block tank built on site is another option, but it's more labour and its joints can crack as the ground moves.

Whatever it's made of, it must be closed and dark, with fine insect mesh on the vents and overflow: open standing water evaporates and breeds mosquitoes.

Rules and permissions
  • Planning. A sheet lying on the ground has no height and no floor area, so it isn't a building, and the trenches are well below the size that needs a declaration. But the land is in zone Ntvb (natural area on a wildlife corridor) of the PLUi Vallée du Lot et du Vignoble (version of 12 March 2026), which aims to avoid sealing natural ground, bans digging out or raising the ground except for permitted works, and bans earthworks "covered by sheeting left visible". Those rules apply even to work that needs no permit. The same document quotes article L111-16 of the planning code, which says a permission can't be refused for systems that retain rainwater for the household's own needs if they fit into the landscape, but that only helps once a permit or declaration is being decided. So ask the mairie first: send a sketch and keep their written answer. A dark sheet helps.
  • Nature. The site is inside the ZNIEFF type 1 "Bois sec de Niaudon", a 113-hectare inventory area noted for its semi-dry limestone grassland, the kind where wild orchids grow. The listing bans nothing by itself and the risk here is low: the sheet and its trench cover about 200 m² of ground right next to the buildings. Do the work in autumn or winter, not in spring or early summer, and leave the trees. The local plan maps no protected hedge, wood, dry grassland or wetland on this spot.
  • Clearing. Cutting grass and bushes isn't forest clearance, so no authorisation from the DDT is needed. Felling trees to make room would be, which is why the sheet goes on the open slope.
  • The fence. Since 2023, fences in natural zones must let wildlife through, but only those more than 150 m from a house (OFB). This one is closer, so a boar-proof fence is allowed. The local plan does ask fences in this zone to leave gaps for small wildlife where possible.
  • Collecting the water. Catching rain on your own land isn't an abstraction under water law; a declaration only applies to rainwater drainage schemes of 1 ha or more.
Why not a borehole

The ground here is karst limestone: water runs through scattered cracks, and the rock above the water table is dry. Every borehole on record nearby is on a valley floor, far lower than the house, and they found water at very different levels: from 118 m above sea level down to below sea level. The springs nearby come out at about 100–110 m. The house is at 229 m, so water is at least about 110 m down and could be much deeper:

Finding water isn't guaranteed: a hole can pass between water-bearing cracks and come up dry. A rough judgement for this site, with a hydrogeologist choosing the spot, is a 1 in 5 chance that the first hole is dry. The cost of the hole alone, if water is found at about 140 m:

Assumes a hydrogeologist and ground survey to choose the spot (€2,000), setting up the rig (€1,000; €300 for a second hole), drilling at €70 a metre, casing and sealing at €40 a metre once water is found, a pumping test (€1,200), and €1,000 to seal a dry hole as the law requires. Every extra 10 m of depth adds about €1,100. Then add about €5,000–9,000 for a pump that can lift water 130 m or more, its solar drive, pipework and treatment.

There's paperwork too: a household borehole must be declared on DUPLOS at least a month before drilling.

Compared with that, the rain sheet and its tank cost a fraction as much, can't come up dry, and uses rain the record shows falls every summer.

Before ordering
  1. Measure the house tank and the solar building tank (see The 10 m³ extra tank, and checking the main tanks). If they hold about 124 m³ of usable water or more, the extra tank isn't needed.
  2. Check the levels on site. The extra tank's inlet must sit below the trench outlet (about 231 m) so the sheet's water runs into it, and there must be room to lift a precast tank in.
  3. Check the slope is still open grass and low scrub. The survey is from April 2022.
  4. Email the mairie or the planning office of the Communauté de communes with a sketch, and keep their reply.
  5. Get two or three quotes for the sheet and underlay.
  6. Plan the work for autumn or winter, so it's ready to collect before summer.
Where else this is done
  • Eden Shale Farm, Kentucky: a 40 × 80 ft PVC pond liner (about 230 m² once laid) on a gentle slope, a trench along the bottom edge into a collection box, and a gravity pipe to a tank. Almost exactly this design.
  • Kangaroo Island, South Australia: three 7 × 100 m plastic runs gave close to 100% runoff; ripples in the plastic cost the most in light rain on hot days. Lessons: clear the vegetation, lay the sheet flat, choose a slope above the storage so it flows by gravity, bury the edges.
  • Wharminda, South Australia: a one-hectare sheet. Lessons: anchor it against wind, and store the water in closed tanks, not an open dam, because of evaporation and algae.
  • Gibraltar collected its water on sheeted slopes for most of the 20th century, and the Canary Islands have done it with plastered slopes (alcogidas) for centuries (La Voz de Lanzarote).
  • Design guidance: University of Kentucky AEN-175 (slopes of 2–10% to one corner, gravel-filled edge ditches) and the US Department of Agriculture's lining standard 521 (EPDM at least 1.14 mm, sheets running down the slope, upper sheets overlapping lower ones).

  1. Rain falls on the roofs. The page uses the rain actually measured each day by Météo-France's rain gauge at Anglars-Juillac, 3 km from the house: a year on average since . One millimetre of rain on one square metre of roof is one litre of water.
  2. Most of it reaches the tanks. Of the rain landing on of collecting surface, is counted as reaching the tanks. The rest is lost wetting the tiles, in the first rinse that washes dirt off the roof, and when gutters and filters spill in heavy downpours.
  3. The tanks store it, up to a limit. They hold of usable water. Rain that arrives when they're already full overflows and is lost, which is why a wet winter can't be saved up for a dry summer.
  4. Guests use it.

The water level carries over from each day to the next, through every day since , wet years and dry. So the results below are what would have happened at this house, with these settings, in the weather that actually fell.

How we checked it
  • The rain gauge agrees with its neighbours. From 2010 to 2025, rain between May and October averaged 384 mm at Anglars, 381 mm at Montcuq, 374 mm at Le Montat and 382 mm at Gourdon, all Météo-France gauges within 31 km.
  • An independent source tells the same story. ERA5, a completely separate reconstruction of the weather from satellites and weather models, averages a year here, against the gauge's (). With the settings on this page it gives . It reads wetter than the gauge in several recent summers, so the gauge is the cautious choice. You can switch to it under Rainfall data and climate.
  • It matches what's been seen at the house. With light use (a few guests for part of the season), the model has the tanks full again by January 2026, which is what happened. With the house full all summer, the same winter would have left them only about a fifth full.
  • You can check it against your own tanks. The tool at the bottom of the page turns a few dipstick readings into this house's real collection and water use, and plugs them in.
What it doesn't capture
  • Rain is measured 3 km away. A single summer storm can soak one village and miss the next, but over months and years the totals agree closely.
  • Occupancy below 100% is spread evenly through the season rather than modelled as empty weeks. The seasonal total is the same; a full fortnight in a dry spell is slightly harder than shown.
  • The weather won't repeat itself exactly. The record covers more than 30 wet and dry years, and the climate setting shifts all of them towards the expected future, but nobody can say which coming years will be dry.
  • Tank size, roof areas and collection efficiency are estimates until they're measured.
  • Only the amount of water is modelled, not its quality.

Which tanker can get to the house

Water weighs a tonne per cubic metre, so the lorry's legal weight limit sets how much it can carry: about 12 m³ on a two-axle lorry, 16 m³ on three axles, 20 m³ on four, and 25 to 30 m³ on an articulated tanker. A bigger load costs much less per cubic metre, because the fixed charge and the drive are the same. An articulated tanker needs a lane at least 3 m wide, 4 m of headroom, firm ground, somewhere to turn, and no weight limits on the road or bridges. The hose reaches about 30 m.

Check it against your own tanks

Measure the depth of water in the tanks with a dipstick, in centimetres, at the same spot each time. The proposed ground sheet is left out of these checks, as it isn't built yet. This assumes the tanks are linked, so the water stands at the same level in each. The page looks up the rain measured at Anglars between your readings.

1. How much rain actually reaches the tanks

Take a reading before and after a rainy spell, when nobody is using water and the tanks aren't full (so nothing overflows). Morning readings line up best with the gauge.

2. How much water guests actually use

Take a reading at the start and end of a stretch with guests in the house, ideally a dry week.

Rainfall: Météo-France (Anglars-Juillac gauge, open data) and ERA5 via Open-Meteo. Data runs , last updated . Everything this page needs is contained in it, so it also works offline.
